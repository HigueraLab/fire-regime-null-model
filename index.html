2. Fire history from fire-scarred trees and sediment-charcoal records

This tab illustrates the challenges of inferring fire history from two kinds of records: fire-scarred trees (tree rings), used mainly in low- to mixed-severity fire regimes where trees survive fire and record it as a scar, and sediment-charcoal records (e.g., lake sediments), used mainly in high- and mixed-severity regimes where fire kills most or many trees. In the model, both records are perfect unless you change the false-negative and false-positive settings below.

The sample points are fire-scarred trees (blue squares). Together they are meant to record the fire history of the sampled area (dashed square): the square spanned by the trees, extended beyond the outer trees by half the distance between trees, so each tree sits at the center of the area it represents. A composite fire year is any year in which at least the chosen number of trees were scarred. The mean interval between composite fire years is the mean composite fire return interval (mCFRI).

Sediment-charcoal record (lake, ×): the center pixel represents a lake whose sediments record every fire that burns that pixel. Cells have no fixed units, so lake size is set relative to the cell size and to fire size: when fires typically span many cells, treating the lake as a single cell is realistic. Lake-sediment records are the main tool for inferring fire history in high- and mixed-severity fire regimes, where fire kills most or many trees and FRPs are generally a century or longer. In low- and mixed-severity regimes with FRPs under about 75 years, tree-ring (fire-scar) records are more useful, and necessary to resolve individual fires.

Rule of thumb: if the FRP is longer than about 100 years, fire scars are unlikely to be the record you rely on; if the FRP is shorter than about 50 years, lake sediments are unlikely to resolve individual fires. The default FRP (100 yr) sits at that boundary, in mixed-severity territory, so both records apply here.

Truth is the fire regime of the sampled area: the expected FRP, which equals the expected mFRI at every point in it. Because fire is patchy, a single fire often burns only part of the sampled area. The composite, however, treats every composite fire year as a fire across the whole sampled area.

  • False positive (▲): a composite fire is registered, but most of the sample area did not burn (for example, a small fire that scarred a few trees, or a scar not caused by fire). False positives make the mCFRI too short.
  • False negative (▼): most of the sample area burned, but no composite fire is registered (too few trees were scarred to pass the minimum, or burned trees did not scar). False negatives make the mCFRI too long.

About the model

A null model generates fire histories in which all variability is random: there are no trends, climate forcing, fire-fuel feedbacks, or spatial gradients. Any pattern in its output arose by chance. That makes it a yardstick for real fire histories.

Rules

  • When: each year, one fire occurs with probability p (at most one fire per year).
  • Where: the fire starts at a random cell on a homogeneous 100 × 100 landscape. Edges wrap around.
  • How big: Realistic mode (recommended) and Simple mode differ here. Simple mode uses square fires with exponentially distributed side lengths. Realistic mode uses elliptical fires (random orientation, length:breadth 1–3) with areas from a truncated power law. Both modes are tuned to the same mean fire size, so the expected FRP is the same: FRP = 1 ÷ (p × mean fire size).

Statistics

  • Fire rotation period (FRP): years ÷ (total area burned ÷ study area). An area-based measure.
  • Mean fire return interval (mFRI): the average time between successive fires at the same point. The app measures every interval between two fires at the same cell (both fires inside the analysis window) and averages all of them across all cells ("pooled"). This is a point-based measure, like a fire-scar study that samples every point on the landscape.
  • Mean composite fire return interval (mCFRI): the average time between fire years recorded by any of the sample trees (or by at least the chosen number of trees).

Fire-scar records, composite fire years, and false positives and negatives

Tab 2 mimics a fire history built from fire-scarred trees, which applies to low- to mixed-severity fire regimes where trees survive fire. A few trees sample an area. Any year in which at least the chosen number of trees were scarred becomes a composite fire year, and the mean interval between composite fire years (mCFRI) is used to estimate the fire regime of the sampled area. Because fires are patchy, a small fire that scarred a few trees is still registered as a composite fire. When most of the sample area did not burn, that is a false positive, which shortens the mCFRI. When most of the sample area burned but too few trees were scarred to pass the minimum (or, if that setting is changed, burned trees did not scar), no composite fire is registered: a false negative, which lengthens the mCFRI. The minimum number of trees recording sets the balance between the two.

On tab 2, both records (fire-scarred trees and sediment charcoal) can be made imperfect with the same two settings: the chance of a false negative (a fire occurs but is not recorded) and the chance of a false positive (a scar or charcoal peak attributed to fire that was not from a fire).

FRP and mFRI: the same in theory

In a uniform fire regime, the FRP and mFRI are equal, provided enough time is sampled. In this model each cell burns in a given year with probability p × (mean fire size), so mFRI = FRP = 1 ÷ (p × mean fire size) exactly.

Estimates from a finite record can differ. FRP estimates vary by chance. The mFRI estimate is also biased low when the record is short relative to the FRP, because intervals longer than the record cannot be observed and long intervals are less likely to fit inside it. With the default record (2000 yr, 20 times the FRP of 100 yr), the expected mFRI estimate is 95% of the FRP; at 10 times, 89%; at 5 times, 76%.

Simplifications

  • No fire-fuel feedbacks: real fire probability often depends on time since the last fire.
  • A homogeneous landscape with geometric fire shapes: topography and fuels do not vary at all, and there are no unburned islands or refugia.
  • Realistic-mode power-law exponents are set by matching the mean fire size, not fitted to data.
  • Our fire-history records record fire perfectly, unless parameters are changed otherwise (e.g., a tree experiences fire but does not leave a fire scar).
